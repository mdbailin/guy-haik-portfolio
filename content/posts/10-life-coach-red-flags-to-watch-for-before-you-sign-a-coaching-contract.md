Looking for someone to guide you can be confusing.

Some people have impressive qualifications. Others have thousands of followers, expensive programs, and testimonials from people who say their lives completely changed. Some promise to help you find your purpose, build confidence, heal your relationships, or finally become the person you always wanted to be.

But how do you know who to trust?

You don’t necessarily need to find a perfect guide. Nobody is perfect, and this is a very personal experience. What works for one person might not work for another. Still, there are things worth paying attention to before you give someone your trust, your time, or your money.

Here are ten red flags I would look out for.

## 1. They promise to transform your life

Be careful when someone guarantees that they can change your life, solve your problems, or bring you a particular result within a specific period.

Inner growth isn’t that predictable. People have different circumstances, responsibilities, personalities and challenges. A guide can offer perspective and help you reflect, but cannot promise how your life will turn out.

What to ask: “What kind of progress can I realistically expect, and how would we work toward it?”

Pay attention to whether the answer is realistic, or sounds like a sales pitch.

## 2. They pressure you to commit right now

You have probably seen offers that expire in a few hours, or claims that you must act now if you are serious about changing your life.

There is nothing wrong with a deadline or a discount. The problem begins when pressure replaces a real conversation about whether this is right for you. You should be able to take time to think. If someone makes you feel that asking for time means you aren’t committed to your own growth, I would be cautious.

What to ask: “Can I take some time to consider this before making a decision?”

A reasonable answer leaves you free to decide without guilt. Even guilt is something to look at calmly. It does not have to decide for you.

## 3. They offer a program before understanding you

Imagine you reach out because you feel stuck in life. Before any real conversation, the person recommends a long, expensive program.

How do they know what you need? Perhaps a longer relationship would help. Perhaps it wouldn’t. The point is that they should understand something about your situation before recommending an arrangement.

What to ask: “Why do you recommend this for my situation, and are there alternatives?”

You don’t have to choose the most expensive option to prove you are serious.

There is a difference between someone who first listens to a need and someone who starts by praising what they sell. When the product comes before the listening, notice it.

## 4. They claim they can help absolutely everyone

Different guides have different backgrounds, experiences and areas of focus. No one is automatically the right person for every person and every problem.

I would be more comfortable with someone who can explain their limitations than with someone who has an answer for everything. A responsible guide recognizes when your needs fall outside their experience, or when another kind of professional support would fit better.

What to ask: “What kinds of problems are outside your area of expertise?”

The answer says a lot about how honestly they approach their work. To me, learning should make you more curious, more humble and more open-minded, not convinced that you are smarter than everyone else.

## 5. Their background and experience are unclear

Guidance of this kind is not a single, universally regulated profession. Training, credentials and standards vary considerably depending on the person and the country.

That doesn’t mean everyone without a long list of certificates can’t help. It does mean you should understand what their background actually is. If someone claims a certification, check what it represents. If they call themselves an expert, ask about the experience behind that claim.

Testimonials can be useful, but they aren’t a substitute for understanding someone’s training and approach. And sometimes a long list of titles says less than one short, honest answer.

What to ask: “Where does your experience come from, and what are you not qualified to do?”

## 6. They don’t welcome questions or disagreement

A relationship like this shouldn’t require you to agree with everything you hear. You might question a suggestion, disagree with an interpretation, or decide that an exercise isn’t useful for you. That is normal.

A guide can challenge you, and a difficult conversation can be valuable. But there is a difference between challenging your thinking and making you feel wrong for questioning at all. If every disagreement is treated as resistance, negativity or a lack of commitment, pay attention.

What to ask: “What happens if I disagree with your advice or don’t find your approach helpful?”

You should be able to have an honest conversation without feeling intimidated.

## 7. They make you feel you can’t manage without them

This is one of the biggest concerns I would have. A guide can be genuinely helpful, and having someone who knows your situation can bring perspective and structure. There is nothing wrong with that.

The problem begins when someone implies that you cannot make decisions, solve problems or keep growing without their ongoing involvement. Be especially careful with demands for obedience, with pressure to cut ties with people close to you, and with the claim that they hold the only truth. You should not feel afraid of ending the relationship.

For me, good guidance should leave you better able to guide yourself afterward. That doesn’t mean you will never need support again. It means that what you learn becomes something you can use on your own.

What to ask: “How do you help people become more independent in their thinking and decisions?”

## 8. They act outside their professional boundaries

Conversations about inner life can touch relationships, emotions, work, confidence and personal struggles. But guidance is not therapy. A guide should not present themselves as qualified to diagnose or treat mental-health conditions unless they hold the relevant professional qualifications and work within their scope.

Be cautious if someone dismisses the need for appropriate professional care, claims they can cure a condition on their own, or pressures you to share things you aren’t comfortable sharing. A guide should also respect your privacy and your right to make your own decisions.

What to ask: “How do you handle situations that fall outside your expertise?”

A responsible answer acknowledges the limits of what a guide can appropriately do. In the view of Maimonides, as I understand it, doing things in moderation leads to better health. He said it about food, but I believe it applies to boundaries too. And if someone says they can cure you no matter what, stay away.

## 9. They use shame or fear to motivate you

Some guides have a direct, challenging style, and some people prefer that. Being challenged isn’t automatically bad, and growth can be uncomfortable. But discomfort alone doesn’t mean you are making progress.

Be cautious if someone regularly humiliates you, calls you weak for struggling, suggests that everything is your fault, or uses your fears to push you into more sessions, more courses or more payments. You can be challenged without being degraded.

What to ask: “How do you respond when I am avoiding something difficult or not following through?”

You want to understand how they handle difficult moments, not only how they behave when everything is going well.

## 10. The practical terms are unclear

Sometimes the warning signs aren’t dramatic. The details are simply missing. You don’t know exactly what you would be paying for, the cancellation terms are vague, and you aren’t sure how long a conversation lasts, how often you would speak, or what happens if you want to stop.

Before committing, understand the fees, payment arrangements, cancellation terms, confidentiality practices and any other conditions. If you are buying a package, find out what happens to unused sessions if you end the arrangement. These questions may not feel exciting, but they prevent misunderstandings later.

What to ask: “What exactly do I pay for, what are the terms, and how do I stop if I want to?”

Money makes the world go round, unfortunately, and it is what it is. For me it isn’t the point, and whatever the arrangement is, I will state it clearly before you send a question or pay anything.

## One red flag doesn’t automatically mean someone is a bad guide

It is important to make a distinction here. Someone might have a style you dislike without doing anything unethical. They might charge more than you are willing to pay. Another might have limited experience in an area that matters to you. None of that automatically makes a person dishonest.

The point isn’t to find a reason to distrust everyone. It is to notice whether the person is transparent, respects your boundaries, communicates honestly, and seems right for the kind of support you are looking for.

You also don’t need to prove that someone is a bad guide before deciding not to work with them. If it doesn’t feel right, you can simply walk away.

## What if you have already started?

If you have already started and you recognize one or more of these signs, don’t panic. Take a step back. Can you raise your concerns? Are they willing to discuss changes? Do you understand your options if you decide to stop?

If the person’s behavior makes you feel unsafe, or you believe you were misled, you don’t have to continue just because you have already invested money or time. Review any agreement, keep records of relevant communication, and seek appropriate consumer or professional advice if necessary. And remember: money you already spent isn’t, by itself, a reason to keep spending more.

I would also be wary of anyone who communicates mainly through contracts and legal paperwork. I prefer to work with someone I trust.

## How do you know if a guide is right for you?

Once you have looked at the red flags, look at the other side. What would you actually like from the person? Perhaps you want someone who challenges your thinking. Perhaps you want space to talk through a difficult decision, or help turning an idea into action. Some people prefer suggestions and perspectives, others prefer someone who mostly asks questions.

There isn’t one approach that works for everyone. Before committing, have a conversation. Ask about their approach, experience, expectations and limitations. Notice whether you can speak honestly, and whether you feel pressured to decide. You don’t need to decide everything in the first conversation.

Also ask whether you speak the same language, in the wider sense: culture, humor, values. When I travel, I try to learn a few words of the local language and something about the culture, and it opens doors. The same is true between people. ## My approach

I believe guidance should be a relationship in which you can think openly, question things, and work toward a better understanding of your own situation. That doesn’t mean every conversation will be comfortable or that I will always agree with you. Sometimes it is useful to challenge an assumption or consider an option you hadn’t thought about.

But I don’t believe a guide should position themselves as the person who has all the answers to your life. You are the one who lives with your decisions. The point is to help you make them with more understanding and confidence. I also think it is important to be honest about whether I am the right person for a particular question. Sometimes another kind of support is the better choice.

## Final thoughts

Choosing someone to guide you takes more than looking at a website, reading testimonials, or listening to promises. Pay attention to how the person communicates, how they respond to questions, whether they respect your boundaries, and whether the practical terms are clear.

Be cautious of pressure, unrealistic guarantees, and anyone who makes you feel that your success depends on staying with them. You don’t need the most famous guide or the most expensive program. You need an approach that fits your situation, a person you can speak to honestly, and a relationship that respects your independence.

And if someone doesn’t feel right for you, you are allowed to say no. That is true of any guide, including me.
