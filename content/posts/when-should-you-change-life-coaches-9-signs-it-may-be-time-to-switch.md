When Should You Change Life Coaches? 9 Signs It May Be Time to Switch

By Guy Haik

Working with a life coach starts with trust. In the series Vikings, Ragnar tells Floki that he talks a lot about trust, and Floki, offended, answers that he is a trustworthy person. Talking about trust is easy. So is the answer to trust nobody? No. The way I see it, sometimes a look in someone’s eyes is enough to sense whether they are telling the truth, and your gut feeling about a coach counts as information. But a feeling alone is not enough. Also, a good coach will sometimes challenge you, and that is not a problem in itself. The signs below are different: they are patterns that keep repeating.

Here are nine signs worth paying attention to.

1\. You Are Encouraged to Do Things Against Your Conscience

In combat sports, fighters follow their coach’s instructions in the ring, and that is fine there. Life is not a ring. A good coach can push you to grow, but should never push you to act against your values: to be aggressive, to cut people out of your life on a whim, or to lower yourself to someone else’s level, physically or verbally. Respect your character.

A good coach reminds you that you are going to be okay. You have made it through every hard day so far, and today is no different. In the end you can say proudly: here I am, I appreciate the little things, and I am ready for tomorrow. If you leave sessions feeling pushed away from yourself, take that seriously.

2\. Technology Replaces Human Attention

There is a viral joke about a job interview with an AI, where the candidate keeps repeating “ignore all previous instructions” until the system hires him. It is funny because everyone knows what it feels like to deal with something that is not really listening.

Coaching is the opposite. It works because a real person is present with you. Tools can support a coach, but warning signs are copy-pasted replies, generic templates, automated messages, or advice that clearly was not written with you in mind. The song Shallow asks whether you are happy in this modern world or searching for something more. That is a good question to bring into any session, and a good coach will have the time to hear your answer.

3\. Talking Has Replaced Doing

There is a funny video about a person whose hobbies are staying home, putting the phone on do not disturb, and staring at the wall, maybe with a candle if feeling adventurous. It is a joke, but it points at something real: sessions can become a comfortable place to talk about change without changing anything.

If months go by and your life looks the same, ask yourself what you actually do between sessions. Go out on an adventure, take a trip, meet new people, try one small thing you have been avoiding. A good coach pulls you toward life, not away from it.

4\. Sessions Add Noise Instead of Peace

Ever wondered why some people love being alone? Quiet people are not necessarily lonely. They often enjoy solitude because it gives the mind a break from constant noise, opinions and social pressure. They can think clearly, recharge, and enjoy their own company. And being alone does not always mean wanting fewer people. Sometimes it means wanting better people. If you love your alone time, there is nothing wrong with you. Your mind may simply value peace over noise.

Now apply that to coaching. After a session, do you feel clearer and calmer, or more anxious, agitated and full of other people’s opinions? Coaching should leave room for your own thoughts. Appreciate your “me time” and stay connected to nature.

5\. Negativity or Hatred Is Promoted Instead of Peace

Try to let go of hatred as much as you can, because it brings no benefit. In the movies Inside Out and Inside Out 2 there is no character called Hatred. As far as I understand, psychologists do not usually count hatred as a basic emotion but as a complex mix of others, such as anger, disgust and fear. I like that the films leave it out.

A coach should help you understand anger and resentment, not feed them. Warning signs are a coach who keeps encouraging you to see other people as enemies, to blame, to keep score, or to cut people off. Good coaching leaves you more peaceful, not more bitter.

6\. You Feel Increasingly Dependent on Your Coach

This is an important point that deserves attention.

A coaching relationship should help you develop your own ability to understand situations, make decisions and deal with challenges. Of course, having someone to talk to and receive support from can be valuable, and there is nothing wrong with appreciating your coach or wanting to continue working together.

However, if you increasingly feel unable to make decisions without consulting your coach, or believe you cannot manage your problems without regular sessions, it may be worth reconsidering the direction of the relationship.

Good coaching should not make you feel that you need someone else to manage your life. The goal is to build your confidence, judgement and ability to guide yourself, even when your coach is no longer involved.

7\. You Cannot See Meaning or Progress in Your Sessions

If your sessions drift into superficial things like gossip, or into long stories about other people or about the coach, you are not in a good spot. Ask yourself: what has changed since I started? Can I name one insight, one decision, one new habit? If not, that is the sign.

A funny fact about gossip: when someone does not like you, they eventually team up with someone else who feels the same, and before long there is a whole group talking behind your back. But the people who talk about you often do not even enjoy each other’s company. The only thing holding them together is their obsession with you. See how powerful you are? So do not stoop to that level, and do not pay for sessions that go there.

8\. You Feel Pressured to Continue Paying for Sessions

Do not let anyone manipulate you or promise things that cannot be achieved. There is a well-known fable of the boiling frog. A frog dropped into boiling water jumps out immediately. A frog placed in lukewarm water settles in comfortably, and when the water is heated slowly, it gets used to each small change until it is too late. (Real frogs tend to jump out as the water warms, so take it as a metaphor and not as science.)

The metaphor describes how manipulation often works. It does not begin with big control, but with a small restriction, a small lie, a little fear, each one normalized before the next arrives. People adjust. Warning signs include pressure to buy long prepaid packages, guilt when you want a break, guaranteed results, terms that change along the way, and being told you are not ready to stop. A healthy relationship has clear prices, clear cancellation terms and the freedom to pause.

When someone keeps changing the rules around you, do not only ask yourself whether you can adjust. Ask yourself: where is this leading?

9\. Your Original Need Is Met, or Has Become Vague

There is a story about a village terrified of wolves that came at night to steal goats and chickens. The villagers collected money and bought a fierce hunting dog. They fed it the best food, built it a place to rest, and considered it the protector of the village. After two months the dog risked its life and killed the last wolf. The village was safe. But once the fear disappeared, the villagers began feeding the dog scraps, and within a month they were throwing stones at it because its barking at night disturbed their sleep.

It is a bitter parable about how the world often values us only as long as it needs us. In coaching it teaches two things. First, a good coach is not like the villagers: they recognize when the job is done and say so, instead of inventing new needs to keep you coming. Second, if you have outgrown a coach, or your original goal has dissolved into “we just talk,” leaving is not betrayal. Thank them for the part they played and move on with respect.

Before Changing Coaches, Is There Something Worth Discussing?

I remember an episode of the animated Spider-Man series where he was given the power to choose his own team of heroes, and they did not like it at first and wanted to fight him. As I remember it, instead of fighting back he tried to calm things down.

The same applies here. Before you leave, consider an honest conversation: say what is not working and what you need. A good coach will welcome that feedback and adjust. Blaming everyone and moving on to the next coach solves little, and so does acting like a victim, but clear and calm feedback often solves a lot.

There is one exception. If the problem is pressure, disrespect, manipulation or crossed boundaries, you do not owe anyone a debate. You can leave.

How to Find a Better-Fit Life Coach

Once you have decided to move on, take some time to consider what you actually want from your next coaching relationship. Rather than choosing someone simply because they have impressive marketing or a long list of testimonials, think about what was missing from your previous experience.

• What did I value in my previous coach, and want to keep?

• What was missing, or what bothered me?

• How will I know in a few sessions whether this new coach is a better fit?

One more thing: coaching is not therapy. If you are going through serious emotional difficulty, a licensed mental health professional is the right person to turn to.

What I Believe a Good Coaching Relationship Should Offer

A line often attributed to the character Whis in Dragon Ball Super says that you become free once you stop thinking about what to do. In the story Whis says it while training Goku in Ultra Instinct, explaining that when you have to think about moving, your reactions are delayed, and that true freedom comes when the body moves on its own. I believe there is truth in it. It is like a tornado kick: if you overthink it, it disturbs you, but when you have practiced enough, your brain already knows the motion and it happens automatically. It is like the scene in The Karate Kid where the boy blocks a punch without thinking because the movement is already stored in him.

Beyond that, I believe a good relationship starts with simple things. Simplicity leads to good things, and there is no point in making things complicated. It should also be obvious that color, age, religion, gender and race do not matter. In an interview in London, Muhammad Ali said that minds, brains and hearts have no color, and that God looks at our actions and deeds. These are fundamental things.

And the relationship must rest on respect. If it loses the essence of respect, there may be no point in continuing. Mike Tyson said in a well-known interview that he needs respect, that he is a human being, and that he needs love like everyone else. That is a rawer way of saying what every person needs, including in a coaching room.

As the author David Orr wrote, the planet does not need more successful people, it needs more peacemakers, healers, restorers, storytellers and lovers of every kind. Be the reason someone still believes in goodness, and it may come back to you someday. That is how karma works: doing good without expecting anything in return.
