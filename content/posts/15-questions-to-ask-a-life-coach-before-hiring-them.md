15 Questions to Ask a Life Coach Before Hiring Them

Choosing a life coach can be harder than it first appears.

The way I see it sometimes all we need is one person in our life, just one who says, "I believe in you. I got you." And if this whole thing goes sideways, I will still be there for you. And that gives you the strength to do whatever needs to be done. You can find plenty of people online who promise to help you build confidence, find direction, improve your relationships, or finally make the changes you've been thinking about for years. Some have impressive qualifications. Others have years of experience or hundreds of testimonials.

But how do you know whether a particular coach is right for you?

You could spend hours comparing websites, reading reviews, and watching introductory videos. Eventually, though, you need to speak to the person you're considering working with.

And when you do, it's worth asking a few questions.

You don't need to turn the conversation into a job interview. The point isn't to catch the coach out or find someone who gives the perfect answer to everything. It's to understand how they work, what they offer, and whether you can see yourself having honest conversations with them. For example, someone who can provide something that can "fill the void" but only for a short period of time. Choose your dopamine hits wisely. Real dopamine: **Enjoying the little things**, New experiences, Good friendships, Good food, Forgiveness, Breathwork, Teamwork, Gratitude, Exercise, Nature, Love, Sun(Vitamin D). Not everything that makes you feel good is good for you.  Some habits give you temporary pleasure, while others bring lasting happiness, peace and fulfillment. Choose what nourishes your mind, not just what satisfies it for a moment. Because true happiness isn't about chasing pleasure. It's about creating a life you don't need to escape from.

3\. What is your coaching approach?

This question matters because coaches can work in very different ways.

Some use structured exercises and goal-setting. Others focus more on conversation, reflection, and helping clients examine their assumptions. Many combine different methods.

Ask the coach to explain what their approach looks like in practice, not just give you a list of impressive-sounding terms.

You should have some idea of what working together might feel like before you commit.

The answer should give you an idea of whether their style is likely to suit you.

10\. How often should we meet?

Some coaching relationships involve weekly sessions. Others work better with meetings every two weeks or at a different frequency.

The right arrangement depends on your goals, availability, budget, and the nature of the work.

Ask what the coach recommends and why. You should also feel able to discuss an arrangement that fits your circumstances.

More frequent sessions aren't automatically better, and you shouldn't feel that you must commit to an intensive schedule to make progress.

12\. Can I stop coaching if it isn't working for me?

You should understand how the coaching relationship can end before it begins.

Ask whether you can stop after an individual session, whether you are committed to a package, and what happens to any unused sessions if you decide not to continue.

Also consider whether you could tell the coach that you weren't satisfied with the experience.

A coach doesn't have to guarantee that every session will be perfect. But you should understand your options if the relationship isn't working.

13\. How do you handle confidentiality and personal information?

Coaching conversations can involve personal matters, so it's important to understand how your information is handled.

Ask whether sessions are recorded, how notes are stored, who can access your information, and what confidentiality arrangements apply.

Don't assume that every coach follows the same policies or that coaching is automatically covered by the same legal confidentiality rules as therapy.

The coach should explain their actual practices and any relevant limits clearly.

14\. What happens if my needs fall outside your expertise?

A life coach shouldn't be expected to solve every problem.

Depending on the situation, someone may need a therapist, a medical professional, a legal adviser, a financial professional, or another specialist rather than, or alongside, a coach.

Ask how the coach recognizes these situations and what they would do if they felt another kind of support was more appropriate.

A willingness to acknowledge limitations is a strength, not a weakness.

What should good coaching leave you with?

There's one final question I think is worth considering, even if you don't ask it directly:

What should I be able to do better because of coaching?

For me, good coaching should leave you better able to guide yourself after the coaching ends.

That doesn't mean you'll never need support again. Nobody has to figure everything out alone, and there is nothing wrong with asking for help when you need it.

But coaching should help you develop a better understanding of your situation, make decisions with greater clarity, recognize patterns, and take action on the things that matter to you.

The goal isn't to keep you paying for sessions indefinitely. It's to make the time you spend coaching worthwhile.
