Choosing someone to guide you can be harder than it first appears.

The way I see it, sometimes all we need is one person in our life, just one, who says: “I believe in you. I’ve got you. And if this whole thing goes sideways, I will still be here.” That gives you the strength to do whatever needs to be done.

You can find plenty of people online who promise to help you build confidence, find direction, improve your relationships, or finally make the changes you have been thinking about for years. Some have impressive qualifications. Others have years of experience or hundreds of testimonials. But how do you know whether a particular person is right for you?

You could spend hours comparing websites, reading reviews, and watching introductory videos. Eventually, though, you need to speak to the person you are considering. And when you do, it is worth asking a few questions.

This isn’t a job interview. The point isn’t to catch anyone out or to find someone with the perfect answer to everything. It is to understand how they work, what they offer, and whether you can see yourself having honest conversations with them.

It also helps to be honest about what you are looking for. Someone can offer something that fills the void, but only for a short period of time. Some things give a quick lift: endless scrolling, instant gratification, procrastination, comfort food, fake relationships. Others nourish you for longer: small pleasures, new experiences, good friendships, forgiveness, gratitude, time in nature, movement, love. Not everything that feels good is good for you. True happiness isn’t about chasing pleasure. It is about creating a life you don’t need to escape from.

So ask yourself: are your daily habits bringing you closer to peace, or just helping you escape for a moment?

Here are fifteen questions I recommend asking, each with my own answer.

## 1. What do you say about taking things personally?

A good guide should have a view on how you relate to other people’s opinions.

My answer: try to take nothing personally. It is the second of the four agreements in Don Miguel Ruiz’s book The Four Agreements, and there is a lot of freedom in it. We spend so much time worrying about what others think of us, when in reality you are the creator of your own life. What someone says about you often says more about them than about you.

## 2. What do you recommend when my mind is agitated?

This tells you whether the person has practical tools, not only ideas.

My answer: there is a calm scene in the film Kung Fu Panda where Master Oogway tells Master Shifu that when the mind is agitated it is hard to see, and that if you let it settle, the answer becomes clear. It is a simple lesson about patience and clarity, and I come back to it often. The most valuable asset you own isn’t money. It is the ability to think clearly in a world designed to confuse you. Before you decide anything important, give your mind time to settle.

## 3. What is your approach?

This matters because guides work in very different ways. Some use structured exercises, others focus on conversation and reflection, and many combine methods. Ask the person to explain what their approach looks like in practice, not just to give you a list of impressive-sounding terms. You should have some idea of what working together might feel like before you commit.

My answer: when you stop wearing the handcuffs of what people think of you, something changes. I am in competition with no one. I have no desire to play the game of being better than anyone. I am simply trying to be better than the person I was yesterday.

The moment you stop comparing your life to others, you start living your own. Someone else’s success doesn’t mean you are falling behind. Everyone has their own journey, their own struggles and their own timing. I don’t need to prove anything to anyone, I know who I am.

If you stopped comparing yourself to others, what would you be most proud of in your own journey?

## 4. Do you give advice, ask questions, or both?

Some people want a guide who mainly asks questions and helps them reach their own conclusions. Others want someone who is willing to share perspectives and challenge their thinking. Neither preference is automatically better. The important thing is to find out how the person handles the balance, and to say what you prefer.

My answer: it depends on the situation. During my studies I learned about analyzing a situation and responding to what it actually needs. I like the idea that when something catches a wise person off guard, their first instinct is to react, like everyone’s. But they don’t follow it. They sit with it for a second, let it pass, and then choose what to do.

Here is a small exercise. Think fast, in three seconds, and name three people you love. Three, two, one. Did you name yourself? Love yourself too. It is not a shame.

## 5. What happens during a typical conversation?

You shouldn’t have to guess what you are getting. Ask how long a conversation usually lasts, how it is structured, and whether you are expected to prepare anything. There isn’t one correct structure, but the person should be able to explain theirs.

My answer: sometimes you will have homework. As a kid I found it difficult to stay concentrated for long, and I started to think I might be stupid. But quitting because you aren’t good enough isn’t an answer. Find a way to overcome obstacles yourself. I believe games make us smarter, so I started solving Rubik’s cubes: first 3x3, and now 6x6, because I understand the concept behind it. It is like using nunchaku. It is easier to control once you understand what the idea is all about. Succeeding at a muscle-up is similar to the game Guitar Hero: it is about timing and how well you are synced with the motion.

I also like fighting, nature and animals, but it isn’t really about me, it is about you. One of Bruce Lee’s best-known sayings is to always be yourself, express yourself and have faith in yourself. As for length and format, they change according to the need.

## 6. How will you help me work out what I am looking for?

Sometimes people arrive knowing exactly what they want. Other times they only know that something isn’t working. Both are reasonable starting points. Ask how the person handles uncertainty, and whether you can use the first conversations to work out what matters most. You shouldn’t have to pretend you know what you want just to begin.

My answer: it is not a bad thing not to know everything, and it is not a bad thing to ask for help. But time is precious. Benjamin Franklin wrote in Poor Richard’s Almanack in 1746 that if you love life, you should not squander time, because time is the stuff life is made of. If you want to set goals together, that is completely fine. If you prefer to do it yourself, that is fine too.

Even people who arrive with clear goals sometimes have no idea what they truly want, while those who say “I have no idea” at the start can save everyone time. There are no rules. And the poet James Russell Lowell wrote that the crime is not failure, but low aim.

## 7. How will we know whether this is helping?

Good guidance should lead to some movement outside the conversation itself. Ask how progress is noticed, and what happens if there is none.

My answer: through tiny improvements, made every day. Strength is built gradually. Nobody is born with it. Your greatest enemy is often inside your own head. When you hit your limit, that is often where it starts. Every wall you face is a question asking how much you want it. I suggest choosing one or two markers together, such as a decision you finally made or a conversation you stopped avoiding, and checking them from time to time.

## 8. What happens if I disagree with you?

You shouldn’t have to agree with your guide about everything. What matters is whether you can discuss differences openly. Ask how the person responds when someone challenges their suggestions, or says that a conversation wasn’t helpful.

My answer: there is a video I like, where a person calmly accepts that someone no longer wants to be with them. Thank you for telling me, he says. What someone likes or doesn’t like isn’t a reflection of me, it is a reflection of what works for them. In the same way, disagreement is useful information.

It is human nature to appreciate something only after losing it. It is a shame, but that is the way it is. And if it isn’t working, sometimes that happens, and it isn’t a shame. Move on and let go. You are not a prisoner of your past. You are a creator of your future.

## 9. How do you approach accountability?

Accountability means different things to different people. For one person it means agreeing on an action and reviewing it next time. For another it means looking at why they keep postponing something they say matters. Ask how the person responds when you don’t follow through, because the answer shows whether their style will suit you.

My answer: you can count on me. Money is something that motivates me, but good karma and legacy motivate me more.

There is a parable that makes me think about this. A man holds a woman’s hand to stop her from falling off a cliff, while a heavy rock crushes him. Below her, a snake waits. She thinks: why can’t he pull a little harder? He thinks: I am in so much pain and I am pulling as hard as I can, why won’t she climb a little harder? Neither can see the pressure the other is under. The moral is that, in work, family, feelings and friendships, we should try to understand each other. A little thought and patience goes a long way. (It is widely shared and its author is unknown.)

## 10. How often should we talk?

Some relationships involve weekly conversations, others work better with a different rhythm. The right arrangement depends on your goals, availability and budget. More frequent isn’t automatically better, and you shouldn’t feel you must commit to an intensive schedule to make progress.

My answer: when I was a private guitar teacher, many students wanted to learn almost daily, and some twice a week. It is like asking someone whether his low kick is good. You are the one doing it, so you need to feel it. I will do my best to adapt to your schedule, and I expect the same from the other side. With written questions there is no schedule at all: you send a question whenever you want.

## 11. What about the money?

Discuss the financial side before committing. Find out whether there is a cost, how payment works, whether there are packages or extra charges, and what the rules are for stopping. You should understand exactly what you are getting before you pay anything.

My answer: I don’t really believe in heavy contracts. I prefer to work with someone I trust, without the paper stuff. The simplest option is the written one: you send me a question through the site and I answer by email, based on what I learned at university, the books I read, street smarts, Judaism studies and my personal outlook. It is also the best option for anyone with a tight budget. It is less like “please walk me home” and more like “give me the details and the map, so I can find my way home myself.”

I believe money isn’t what drives me, and the point is to help others. Whatever the arrangement, I will state it clearly before you send a question or pay anything.

## 12. Can I stop if it isn’t working for me?

You should understand how a relationship can end before it begins. Ask whether you can stop at any time, whether you are committed to anything in advance, and whether you can tell the person you weren’t satisfied. Nobody can promise every conversation will be perfect, but you should know your options.

My answer: feel free to tell me how you feel about each answer. Feedback means a lot to me. I always strive to improve, because this is who I am. If you feel it is not a match, you are free to go. I will do my best that it won’t happen, but I can’t control everything, and if things don’t show up the way both sides expected, maybe it is a sign there is no point in keeping it up.

## 13. How do you handle confidentiality and personal information?

Conversations about inner life can involve very personal matters. Ask how information is stored, who can see it, whether anything is recorded or published, and what the limits are. Don’t assume that every guide follows the same practices, or that this kind of conversation has the same legal confidentiality as therapy.

My answer: “What happens in Vegas stays in Vegas” is a famous slogan from a 2003 Las Vegas tourism campaign, and a character repeats it in the film The Hangover. I believe the idea applies in general: it isn’t good to push into someone else’s space, and I would respect it if you wish to keep something secret.

In practice: questions sent through this site go through Google Forms. I publish a question only if you tick the box that allows it, anonymously, and I may remove or change identifying details first. If you leave an email, I use it only to reply. You can read more in the [privacy policy](https://guyhaik.com/privacy.html).

## 14. What if my needs fall outside your expertise?

Nobody can solve every problem. Depending on the situation, someone may need a therapist, a doctor, a lawyer, a financial adviser or another specialist, instead of or alongside a guide. Ask how the person recognizes these situations and what they would do. A willingness to acknowledge limitations is a strength, not a weakness.

My answer: I once told a psychologist about serious money trouble I was facing. He said I was welcome to share anything, but that he wasn’t an expert in financial advice. I respected that. It is the same with me: I am not into finance or the stock market, and I am not a therapist. I can try to help, but a good guide knows where their knowledge ends. If you are struggling with your mental health or are in crisis, please contact a licensed professional or local emergency services.

When I arrived in New Zealand, I had no plan for how to make money. I thought of doing what I had always done, private guitar teaching and teaching music at a local school, and then learned I would need a certificate even for that. Check what is actually required before you rely on an assumption.

There is also a difference between a problem and a paradox. A problem can be solved, a paradox can’t. As long as it is a problem that can be solved, don’t be shy about looking weird or different, and if someone laughs at you, let’s not stoop to their level.

## 15. How will I know whether we’re a good fit?

This question brings everything together. You might agree with someone’s philosophy but find their communication style doesn’t work for you. You might appreciate their experience but want a more direct approach. That is why a first conversation can be useful. Ask what they look for in a productive relationship, and whether you can speak before making any commitment.

My answer: I follow people I admire. There is a short list of those who earned the title of legend. Bruce Lee’s philosophy reminds us that real strength begins with awareness, adaptability and control over yourself. If that speaks to you, we will probably be able to proceed.

Don’t just listen to the answers. Pay attention to how they are given. Does the person listen to what you are saying? Can they explain their approach without hiding behind jargon? Are they willing to say what they don’t know? Do you feel comfortable asking questions or expressing a different opinion? You don’t need an instant connection, but you should have some sense of whether you could speak honestly with this person.

Be especially careful with these red flags: demands for obedience, pressure to cut ties with people close to you, large payments, and claims to hold the only truth. A friendly personality isn’t a substitute for competence, just as impressive credentials don’t guarantee you will work well together. Look at the whole picture. The way I see it, a narrow way of thinking can lead to bad things.

## What should good guidance leave you with?

There is one final question worth considering, even if you don’t ask it directly: what should I be able to do better because of this?

For me, good guidance should leave you better able to guide yourself afterward. That doesn’t mean you will never need support again. Nobody has to figure everything out alone, and there is nothing wrong with asking for help when you need it. But it should help you understand your situation, decide with more clarity, recognize patterns, and act on the things that matter to you.



## One last thought: when to walk away

When it comes to emotional intelligence, one of the greatest signs of strength is knowing when to speak and when to walk away. In a world where everyone wants to be right, winning an argument can feel more important than understanding another person. But how often does an argument truly change someone’s mind, and how much peace do we sacrifice trying to prove something to someone who has already decided not to listen?

Before entering an argument, take a moment to observe the person in front of you. Are they interested in understanding your perspective? Can they listen without becoming defensive? Are they willing to reconsider when given a reasonable explanation? If the answer is no, ask yourself what you hope to achieve by continuing.

Not every disagreement deserves your energy, and not every criticism requires a response. This doesn’t mean avoiding every difficult conversation. Healthy disagreements can help us grow and reveal perspectives we never considered. The difference lies in the willingness to listen. When neither person is willing to listen, the conversation becomes a competition fueled by ego.

You can’t control how other people think, speak or behave. You can control how much power their behavior has over your peace of mind. Sometimes walking away isn’t weakness. It is emotional discipline. Protecting your peace doesn’t mean believing you are always right. It means having enough humility to learn when a conversation is worthwhile, and enough confidence to leave when it isn’t.

What do you think? Is walking away from an unnecessary argument a sign of wisdom, or should we always try to make others understand our perspective?
