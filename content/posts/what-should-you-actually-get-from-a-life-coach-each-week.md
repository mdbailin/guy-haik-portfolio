What Should You Actually Get From a Life Coach After Each Session?

If you're paying for life coaching, it's reasonable to wonder what you're supposed to get out of each session.

You spend an hour talking about your life, your problems, or something you've been trying to figure out. The conversation might be interesting, and you might even leave feeling better. But a day or two later, you find yourself wondering what actually came out of it.

Did anything change? Are you supposed to do something before the next session? And how do you know whether you're making progress or simply talking about the same things every week?

These are fair questions. Coaching takes time, money, and effort, and you should have some idea of what you're getting from it.

The answer isn't that every session needs to end with a completed worksheet or a list of tasks. Sometimes the most useful thing you take away is a clearer understanding of yourself or a decision you finally feel ready to make.

But the conversation should have a purpose. And over time, you should be able to see how your sessions relate to what you're trying to change in your life. What should you expect from life coaching?

There's no single format that every coach follows. Some coaches use structured exercises and goal-setting frameworks, while others take a more flexible approach depending on what's happening in the client's life.

What matters is that you understand what you're working toward and why you're having these conversations.

You might come to a session wanting to discuss a difficult decision. You might be trying to understand why you keep repeating a particular pattern, or you may simply know that something in your life isn't working the way you want it to.

A useful session gives you space to explore what's happening, look at it from different perspectives, and consider what you want to do about it.

That doesn't mean your coach has to solve the problem for you. In fact, the point isn't necessarily to get someone else to tell you what to do. It's to develop a better understanding of your situation and make decisions that you can stand behind.

The important thing is that you aren't left wondering indefinitely why you're having the conversation in the first place. What should happen during a coaching session?

A coaching session should relate to something that matters to you, but it doesn't always have to follow a rigid plan.

You might begin by discussing something that has happened since your last meeting. Perhaps you've tried something new, encountered a difficulty, or changed your mind about a decision.

From there, the conversation might explore what's making the situation difficult, what assumptions you're making, or what options you haven't considered.

Sometimes you'll discover that the problem isn't quite what you thought it was. You might arrive wanting help with one decision and realise that something else is making it difficult to move forward.

That's not necessarily wasted time. Understanding the real issue can be more useful than rushing into a solution.

At other times, the conversation may be much more practical. You might work through your options, decide what matters most, and identify a step you're willing to take.

Not every session needs to produce a major breakthrough. But you should be able to understand how the conversation connects to your concerns, goals, or personal development. Should your life coach give you homework?

Not necessarily.

Some coaches use exercises, written reflections, or specific tasks between sessions. These can be useful when they relate directly to what you're working on.

For example, you might agree to try a different approach to a situation, pay attention to a recurring habit, or take one practical step toward a goal.

But homework isn't proof that coaching is working. You can complete every exercise your coach gives you without making meaningful changes, and you can make progress without filling out a single worksheet.

What matters is whether anything you do between sessions helps you understand your situation, test an idea, or move toward a goal.

If your coach suggests an activity, you should understand why it's relevant. And if the activity doesn't suit you, it's reasonable to discuss that rather than doing it simply because you've been told to.

Coaching should help you take responsibility for your own development, not turn your life into a series of assignments you feel obliged to complete. What should happen between sessions?

The conversations are only one part of the experience. Much of what you learn becomes useful when you start applying it to your everyday life.

You might try something you discussed with your coach and discover that it works better than you expected. You might find that it doesn't work at all, or that you encounter a difficulty you hadn't anticipated.

Either way, there's something to learn from the experience.

When you return for your next session, you can talk about what happened, what you noticed, and whether you want to approach things differently.

This is one way to connect individual conversations to longer-term progress. Instead of starting from scratch every time, you can build on what you've learned and adjust your approach as your circumstances change.

What if every session feels vague?

If you regularly leave coaching sessions unsure what you got out of them, it's worth paying attention.

One session that feels inconclusive doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong. Some conversations take time to make sense of, and not every problem has an immediate solution.

But if you repeatedly discuss the same issues without gaining clarity, taking action, or understanding what you're working toward, you shouldn't feel that you have to accept it as normal.

Start by bringing it up with your coach.

You could say something like:

"I'm finding our conversations interesting, but I'm not sure how they're helping me move forward. Can we look at what I'm trying to achieve and how we'll know whether I'm making progress?"

That isn't an unreasonable demand. It's a fair question about the purpose of the work you're paying for.

Your coach may help you clarify your goals, reconsider the approach, or identify something that hasn't been addressed. You might also discover that your expectations differ and need to be discussed.

If nothing changes after you've raised your concerns, it's reasonable to reconsider whether the arrangement is useful for you.

How do you know whether you're getting value from coaching?

Rather than judging every session on its own, it can help to look at the bigger picture.

Think about why you started coaching. What were you hoping would change? What were you struggling with, and what did you want to understand or do differently?

Then look at what's happened since.

Have you made a decision you were previously avoiding? Are you approaching a familiar problem differently? Have you become clearer about your priorities? Are you taking steps toward something that matters to you?

Your progress might not be dramatic, and it may not happen in a straight line. You might move forward in one area while finding another more difficult.

That's why it helps to discuss progress with your coach rather than relying only on how you feel immediately after a session.

You should be able to talk about what's helping, what isn't, and what you want to focus on next.

And remember that value doesn't necessarily mean needing more sessions. Sometimes the most useful outcome is realising that you have what you need to move forward independently.
