You have been talking with someone about what is happening in your life, and trying to make progress. But after a while you start asking a question you didn’t expect to ask: is any of this actually helping?

Maybe you are having the same conversations over and over. Perhaps you leave feeling positive but struggle to do anything differently afterward. Or maybe you simply can’t tell whether you are getting closer to what made you look for guidance in the first place. It is frustrating, especially when you have invested time and sometimes money.

But guidance that isn’t working doesn’t necessarily mean you chose the wrong person. Sometimes the goals need to be clarified, the approach needs to change, or expectations need to be reconsidered. In other cases, the relationship really isn’t a good fit.

It reminds me of complicated relationships with parents. “Honor your father and your mother” is one of the Ten Commandments, but circumstances sometimes force a person to step away. It seems contradictory, right? What I take from it is that sometimes you need a siesta, a short pause to regain strength before deciding what to do. A pause is not an ending.

Here are eight signs worth paying attention to, each with a practical step you can take before deciding what to do next.

## 1. You are having the same conversation every time

If you feel that you and your guide are just repeating things you already know, it may be time to explore what you don’t know yet. Saying “I don’t know” is a perfectly fine starting point, but it can’t be the destination. A good guide helps turn “I don’t know” into a question you can explore. Sometimes a repeated topic is a sign that you are circling around the core and avoiding it.

What you can do: name the loop out loud. Say, “We keep returning to this,” and ask what you haven’t talked about yet.

## 2. You don’t know what you are working toward

In *Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland*, Alice reaches a fork and asks the Cheshire Cat which way she ought to go. He answers that it depends a good deal on where she wants to get to, and when she says she doesn’t much care, he says it doesn’t matter which way she goes.

If you haven’t found what you are searching for yet, that is completely fine. It happens to everyone. What matters is to keep searching and not settle. But a relationship like this needs at least a direction, even a rough one.

What you can do: write one or two sentences about what you want from these conversations, share them, and ask your guide to say them back to you.

## 3. You don’t feel comfortable being honest

If you are hiding your real doubts, nothing deep can happen. If you feel judged, rushed or afraid to tell the truth, the relationship may not be safe enough. And it works in both directions: repeat a polite version of your story long enough and it starts to feel real, and you drift further from what is actually going on. Have the courage to say the truth as it is.

What you can do: say one true sentence you have been holding back, and notice how your guide reacts.

## 4. The style doesn’t suit you

I like to compare styles of guidance to fighting styles, or to programming languages. If you learn one well, say Java, you learn the others more easily, because you understand the concept. Still, it depends on the person and on how much effort they put in. Some people want structure and firm accountability. Others need slow, reflective conversation. Neither is better. What matters is that the style helps you move, and doesn’t make you feel smaller.

I follow people I admire, and I believe that when you admire someone you tend to imitate them. That can be inspiring, but a guide shouldn’t ask you to become a copy of anyone, including themselves.

What you can do: describe the style you want (direct or gentle, structured or open) and ask whether your guide can adapt to it.

## 5. Nothing changes between conversations

Maybe the conversations feel good, but nothing follows them. Insight without action is just a pleasant conversation. A good process ends with something small and concrete to try.

A muscle-up is a good example. When someone makes it look easy, it is a sign that their mind has understood what it takes and the movement has become smooth. Progress feels like that: less struggle, more precision. If you get inspiration but no next step, the process lacks follow-through.

What you can do: ask for one small action after each conversation, and review it together at the start of the next.

## 6. Your concerns aren’t taken seriously

If someone tells you “just take it easy,” sometimes they are right. But a guide who brushes off your worries, minimizes them, or answers everything with “just be positive” is not listening. What looks small to someone else may be the whole world to you. A concern that keeps returning deserves attention, not dismissal.

Calm under pressure is a skill you can build for yourself. It is not a reason to swallow your concerns because someone told you to.

What you can do: say, “I want to come back to something that worries me,” and see whether your guide stays with it.

## 7. You can’t tell whether you are making progress

You know better than anyone whether you feel better or not, and your intuition is a good guide. Still, intuition works better with a few markers. Progress rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a decision you finally made, a conversation you stopped avoiding, or a night you slept better.

Maybe it is also a never-ending story, in the positive sense. You are still on a journey of self-discovery, so stay curious. If you lose curiosity, what will drive you to keep looking?

What you can do: choose two or three markers, such as sleep, mood, or one decision you have been avoiding, and look at them every four to six conversations. If nothing has moved, say so.

## 8. You feel pressured to keep paying or keep coming back

Someone can take advantage of your vulnerability to keep you in touch and keep you paying. Warning signs include pressure to sign up for long packages, guilt when you want a break, guaranteed results, being told you are not ready to stop, and messages that make you anxious when you don’t reply. A healthy relationship has clear pricing, clear terms, and the freedom to pause.

One more note. If you notice that you panic when conversations end, or need constant proof that the other person cares, bring it up. It may be worth exploring with a licensed therapist, because guidance is not therapy. And a good guide will never use that vulnerability to keep you coming back.

What you can do: ask for the terms in writing, and give yourself a few days before committing to anything long.

## Should you switch, or try a different approach first?

Often the problem isn’t the person, but a deeper layer of communication, expectations or method. Before making a dramatic move like ending the relationship, peel back the outer layers to understand what is actually going wrong.

Before you decide, ask yourself three questions:

- Can I describe what is wrong in one sentence?
- Have I told my guide?
- Did anything change after I did?

If you spoke up and nothing improved, switching is a reasonable choice. If you want more signs to look for, see [my article on when to change guides](post.html?slug=when-should-you-change-life-coaches-9-signs-it-may-be-time-to-switch).

## How to find someone who is a better fit

I would look for someone who values simplicity, and who shows you that rest is a necessity and not a reward. You don’t need to prove you are tired or hit a wall before you slow down. Take a break before you break.

I would also look for someone who sheds light on things that seem obvious but have a reason behind them. And for me, the most beautiful people I know are the ones who are the most authentically themselves. Look for someone who leaves room for you to be you, not a copy of them.

A few practical questions to ask someone new:

- How do you usually work, and what does a conversation look like?
- How will we know whether this is working?
- What are the terms, and how do we end if it doesn’t fit?

## A note on my approach

My point is this: learn to be alone and independent. It is a skill few people master. The stigma says young people are spoiled and can’t manage on their own. Prove them wrong.

Save some money every week, it doesn’t matter how much. Listen to your parents’ advice when you can, since in most cases they want the best for you, though I know that is not true for everyone and some families are complicated. Choose your friends wisely, because you are shaped by your environment. Surround yourself with well-educated people, and even more with well-mannered ones. Read, read and read some more. Look after your body. And don’t wait for someone to love you: learn to love yourself first.

## If it isn’t working, you have options

You can clarify your goals, change the approach, talk openly with your guide, take a pause, or move on to someone who fits better. If what you are going through feels heavy, a licensed therapist may be the right next step.
