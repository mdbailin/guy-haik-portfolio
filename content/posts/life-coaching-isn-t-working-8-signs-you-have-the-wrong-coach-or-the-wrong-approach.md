Life Coaching Isn’t Working: 8 Signs You Have the Wrong Coach or the Wrong Approach

By Guy Haik

You’ve been showing up to your coaching sessions, talking about what is happening in your life, and trying to make progress. But after a while you start asking a question you didn’t expect to ask: is any of this actually helping?

Maybe you are having the same conversations over and over. Perhaps you leave sessions feeling positive but struggle to do anything differently afterward. Or maybe you simply can’t tell whether you are getting closer to the goals that made you seek coaching in the first place. It is frustrating, especially when you have invested both time and money.

But coaching that isn’t working doesn’t necessarily mean you chose the wrong coach. Sometimes the goals need to be clarified, the approach needs to change, or expectations need to be reconsidered. In other cases, the relationship really isn’t a good fit.

It reminds me of complicated relationships with parents. “Honor your father and your mother” is one of the Ten Commandments, but what happens when circumstances force a person to walk away? It seems to contradict, right? In the book and film Into the Wild, Christopher McCandless decided to cut his family out of his life and even destroyed his ID and credit cards to start over under a new name. His story ended in tragedy, so I don’t offer it as a model. But it shows how strongly a person can feel the need for distance. There is a gentler version of the same need: sometimes you need a siesta, a short pause to regain strength before deciding what to do. A pause is not an ending.

Here are eight signs worth paying attention to, along with a practical step you can take for each one before deciding what to do next.

1\. You’re Having the Same Conversation Every Session

If you feel that you and your coach are just repeating things you already know, it may be time to dive into what you don’t know yet. Saying “I don’t know” is a perfectly fine starting point, but it can’t be the destination. A good coach helps turn “I don’t know” into a question you can explore.

Bruce Lee wrote that the path is not daily increase but daily decrease: hack away the unessential. Sometimes a repeated topic is a sign that you are circling something unnecessary and avoiding the core. The Tao Te Ching, the classic text attributed to Lao Tzu and an influence on Bruce Lee, makes a similar point: less effort, aimed well, often does more.

What you can do: name the loop out loud. Say, “We keep returning to this,” and ask what you haven’t talked about yet.

2\. You Don’t Know What You’re Working Toward

The Lion King has a famous song about having no worries for the rest of your days. It is a lovely idea, but it is not a plan. In Alice in Wonderland, Alice reaches a crossroads and asks the Cheshire Cat which way to go. He asks where she wants to get to, and when she says she doesn’t much care, he answers that then it doesn’t matter which way she goes. And in The Lion King itself, after Simba has spent years living carefree, his father appears in the sky and tells him to remember who he is.

If you haven’t found what you are searching for yet, that is completely fine. It happens to everyone. What matters is to keep searching and not settle. But a coaching relationship needs at least a direction, even a rough one.

What you can do: write one or two sentences about what you want from coaching, share them, and ask your coach to say them back to you.

3\. You Don’t Feel Comfortable Being Honest

Bruce Lee is often quoted as saying that if you don’t want to slip up tomorrow, you should speak the truth today. Whether or not he said it, it is good advice, and it works in both directions. If you are hiding your real doubts from your coach, nothing deep can happen. If you feel judged, rushed or afraid to tell the truth in sessions, the relationship may not be safe enough.

Michael Jackson’s Billie Jean has a line about how a lie can become the truth. Repeat a polite version of your story long enough and it starts to feel real, and you drift further from what is actually going on. Have the courage to say the truth as it is.

What you can do: say one true sentence you have been holding back, and notice how your coach reacts.

4\. The Coaching Style Doesn’t Suit You​

I like to compare coaching styles to fighting styles, or to programming languages. If you learn one perfectly, say Java, you learn the others more easily, because you understand the concept. But Bruce Lee said he didn’t believe in styles. It reflects emotional intelligence to understand that it depends on the person and on how much effort they put in. Some people want structure and tough accountability. Others need slow, reflective conversation. Neither is better. What matters is that the style helps you move, and doesn’t make you feel smaller.

I follow people I admire, such as Bruce Lee and Bob Marley, and I believe that when you admire someone you tend to imitate them. That can be inspiring, but a coach shouldn’t ask you to become a copy of anyone, including themselves. If you like the content I write, feel free to get in touch.

What you can do: describe the style you want (direct or gentle, structured or open) and ask your coach whether they can adapt to it.

5\. Nothing Changes Between Sessions

Maybe the sessions feel good, but nothing follows them. Insight without action is just a pleasant conversation. A good coaching process ends each session with something small and concrete to try.

Bruce Lee’s advice to be like water, adapting and flowing around obstacles, and the Taoist idea of Wu Wei, effortless action, point to the same principle: face challenges with minimal, well-timed effort rather than brute force. A muscle-up is a good example. When someone makes it look easy, it is a sign that their mind has understood what it takes and the movement has become smooth. Progress feels like that: less struggle, more precision. If your sessions give you inspiration but no next step, the process lacks follow-through.

What you can do: ask for one small action after each session, and review it together at the start of the next.

6\. Your Concerns Aren’t Taken Seriously

If someone tells you “just take it easy,” sometimes they are right. But a coach who brushes off your worries, minimizes them, or answers everything with “just be positive” is not listening. There is a saying that when we have health we have a thousand wishes, and when we lose it, health becomes the only wish. What looks small to someone else may be the whole world to you.

There is a legend in Chabad tradition about Moshe Meisels, a disciple of Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, who is said to have spied for the Russians against Napoleon. The story goes that Napoleon suspected him, pinned him against a wall, put a hand over his heart to feel his pulse and accused him of spying. Meisels, raised on the idea that the mind must rule the heart, stayed calm, and Napoleon let him go. Details vary from telling to telling, so take it as tradition rather than established history. I mention it because it shows that you can train your mind. But calm under pressure is a skill you build for yourself. It is not a reason to swallow your concerns because someone told you to. A concern that keeps returning deserves attention, not dismissal.

What you can do: say, “I want to come back to something that worries me,” and see whether your coach stays with it.

7\. You Can’t Tell Whether You’re Making Progress

You know better than anyone else whether you feel better or not, and nowadays your intuition is the best guide you have. Still, intuition works better with a few markers. Progress rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a decision you finally made, a conversation you stopped avoiding, or a night you slept better.

Maybe it is also a never-ending story, in the positive sense. You are still on the journey of self-discovery, so stay curious. If you lose curiosity, what will drive you to investigate? People have always wondered how the Egyptian pyramids were built, or how the giant moai statues of Easter Island, carved between roughly 1250 and 1500 CE, were moved to their coastal platforms. Oral traditions and modern experiments suggest the statues were carefully “walked” into place with ropes and synchronized rocking. That kind of curiosity is exactly what keeps a person growing.

What you can do: choose two or three markers, such as sleep, mood, or one decision you have been avoiding, and look at them every four to six sessions. If nothing has moved, tell your coach.

8\. You Feel Pressured to Keep Paying

Someone can take advantage of your vulnerability to keep you in touch and keep you paying. Warning signs include pressure to sign up for long packages, guilt when you want a break, guaranteed results, being told you are not ready to stop, and messages that make you anxious when you don’t reply. A healthy relationship has clear pricing, clear cancellation terms and the freedom to pause.

A note on attachment. People who constantly need reassurance, who worry when someone doesn’t reply or panic when closeness feels uncertain, are often described in psychology as having an anxious attachment style. It does not mean they are needy or weak. It describes a pattern shaped by earlier experiences, often inconsistent care in childhood, and people with it usually love deeply while fearing the loss of the connection. It is not a diagnosis, and I am not in a position to make one. But if you notice this pattern in how you relate to your coach, for example panic when sessions end or a need for constant proof that they care, bring it up. It may be worth exploring with a licensed therapist, because coaching is not therapy. And a good coach will never use that vulnerability to keep you coming back.

What you can do: ask for the terms in writing, and give yourself a few days before committing to anything long.

Should You Switch Coaches or Try a Different Approach First?

Often the problem isn’t the coach itself, but a deeper layer of communication, expectations or method. Before making a dramatic move like ending the relationship, peel back the outer layers to understand what is actually going wrong.

It reminds me of Shrek telling Donkey that ogres are like onions: they have layers. The point is that people are more complex and sensitive inside than their outward appearance suggests. Everyone has layers to peel back on the journey of self-discovery.

The Tai Chi teacher T. T. Liang is quoted as saying that if you rely entirely on the teacher, you will never find the treasure. A good teacher can guide you and explain the principles, but you have to discover their meaning through your own practice. The same is true of coaching, and some of the most valuable moments come when you practice on your own.

Before you decide, ask yourself three questions:

• Can I describe what is wrong in one sentence?

• Have I told my coach?

• Did anything change after I did?

If you spoke up and nothing improved, switching is a reasonable choice. If you want more signs to look for, see my article on when to change life coaches.

How to Find a Coach Who Is a Better Fit

I would look for someone who values simplicity, and who shows you that rest is a necessity and not a reward. You don’t need to prove you are tired or hit a wall before you slow down. Take a break before you break.

I would also look for someone who sheds light on things that seem obvious but have a reason behind them, because everything happens for a reason. And for me, the most beautiful people I know are the ones who are the most authentically themselves. Look for a coach who leaves room for you to be you, not a copy of them.

A few practical questions to ask a new coach:

• How do you usually work, and what does a session look like?

• How will we know whether this is working?

• What are the terms, and how do we end if it doesn’t fit?

A Note on My Approach to Coaching

My point is this: learn to be alone and independent. It is a skill few people master. The stigma says kids nowadays are spoiled and can’t manage on their own. Prove them wrong. A song by Jared Evan celebrates staying kids forever and wanting to play. I like the spirit, as long as it comes with responsibility.

Save some money every week, it doesn’t matter how much. Listen to your parents’ advice when you can, since in most cases they want the best for you, though I know that is not true for everyone and some families are complicated. Choose your friends wisely, because you are shaped by your environment.

Psychologists have long debated which has more power, nature or nurture. Modern science sees development as a continuous interaction between the two, like yin and yang, not a contest with one winner. So surround yourself with well-educated people, and even more with well-mannered ones. Read, read and read some more. Look after your body. And don’t wait for someone to love you: learn to love yourself first.

If Life Coaching Isn’t Working, You Have Options

You can clarify your goals, change the approach, talk openly with your coach, take a pause, or move on to someone who fits better. If what you are going through feels heavy, a licensed therapist may be the right next step.

Keep searching for tools that make the journey meaningful. A message often attributed to the Dalai Lama says that whether you believe in God, in Buddha or in reincarnation matters less than living a good life, and that a good life is not only good food, clothes and shelter. What is needed is a good motivation: compassion, without dogmatism, and respect for the rights and dignity of others.

If you are unsure whether your coaching is working, or what you would want from the next step, I would be glad to talk.
