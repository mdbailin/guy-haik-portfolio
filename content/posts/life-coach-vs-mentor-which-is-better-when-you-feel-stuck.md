You feel stuck.

You know something needs to change, but you aren’t sure what the next step should be. So you start looking for someone who can help, and you come across two words that seem quite similar: mentor and spiritual guide.

Which one do you actually need?

The answer depends on what kind of help you are looking for. For me it is like asking who wins a fight between a professional boxer and an MMA fighter. The answer is: it depends. On what? On the rules. In MMA you can take someone down and choke him, and a boxer is not allowed to. Change the surface and you change the odds dramatically. It is the same here. The question isn’t which one is stronger, but which setting you are standing in right now.

## What is a mentor?

A mentor is usually someone who has experience in an area you want to understand better. They may have already done what you are trying to do. Maybe:

- You want to change careers and find someone who has worked in that industry for twenty years.
- You are starting a business and want advice from someone who has already built one.
- You are developing a skill and want someone who has mastered it.

A mentor can say: “I’ve been there. This is what worked for me.” That kind of experience can be extremely valuable. You don’t always need to discover everything for yourself. Sometimes you simply want to learn from someone who has already made the mistakes.

A title doesn’t always reflect results, though. Don’t rely on dictionary definitions. “It depends on the person” appears in so many places for a reason.

## What is a spiritual guide?

A guide is generally less about having the answers and more about helping you work through your own situation. A guide may help you clarify what you want, examine the choices in front of you, notice what is holding you back, and reflect on your values.

A guide doesn’t need to have lived your exact life. In fact, they probably haven’t. You may be dealing with something completely unique to you, and the guide’s role can be to help you think about it more clearly.

## “But I don’t want someone who just asks me questions.”

This is a reasonable concern. There is a stereotype of a guide who answers everything with another question: “What do you think?” “How does that make you feel?” Sometimes those questions are useful. Sometimes you think: “I don’t know. That’s why I’m talking to you.”

I understand that. I don’t think a guide should be afraid to contribute to the conversation. There are times when a question is the right thing, and times when another perspective, a suggestion, or a challenge is more useful. The important thing is knowing the difference. And it is healthy for you to ask questions and stay a little skeptical, including about me.

## Advice isn’t always the answer either

Imagine you tell a mentor: “I hate my job. Should I quit?” They might tell you what they would do. That could be useful. But their situation isn’t yours. They have different responsibilities, experiences, personality, finances, relationships and priorities. What worked for them might not work for you.

This is where a guide can help. Instead of immediately telling you what to do, a guide can help you understand why you are considering leaving, what you want from your next job, what keeps you where you are, and what risks you are willing to take. You may reach the same conclusion, but it becomes your decision.

A well-known psychology experiment shows how strong the pull of a group can be. In the 1950s, Solomon Asch showed people cards with lines and asked which lines matched. When the others in the room confidently gave an obviously wrong answer, many participants went along with it at least once. I take two things from this: don’t follow the majority just because it is the majority, and have faith in your own uniqueness. It is easy to say and hard to do.

## When might a mentor be the better choice?

A mentor can be especially useful when you want practical knowledge from someone who has walked a similar path. For example:

- You want to enter a particular profession.
- You are starting a business.
- You want to develop a specific skill.
- You are entering a new industry.
- You want to understand how a certain career really works.

If you are asking “How did you do this?”, a mentor may be exactly what you need. And the person who gives you the support that fits can be anyone with wisdom. It doesn’t need a title.

## When might a spiritual guide be the better choice?

A guide can be useful when the problem isn’t a lack of information. Maybe you already know your options, but you don’t know which one you want. Or you know what you want and keep putting it off. You might be dealing with:

- A major life transition.
- A difficult decision.
- A lack of direction.
- Goals you haven’t been able to follow through on.
- Uncertainty about what you actually want.

In these situations another person’s experience may not give you the answer. You may need help understanding your own situation.

## What if you want both?

You don’t have to choose one forever. You might have a mentor who helps you understand your profession, and a guide you talk to about the inner questions. Or you might find one person who combines elements of both.

The labels matter less than the relationship and the kind of help you receive. And if you can have both, why compromise?

## What do you actually want from the person helping you?

Before choosing, ask yourself a few questions:

- Do I want someone to give me advice?
- Do I want someone to ask me difficult questions?
- Do I want someone to share their own experience?
- Do I need help making a decision?
- Do I need someone who will challenge my thinking?
- Do I want someone who has already done what I am trying to do?

Also ask whether you speak the same language, in the wider sense. Are you into philosophy? Do you believe in the spiritual side, in karma? Do you think games make us smarter? Do you read, play music, train, learn new languages? Your answers say a lot about who you will connect with. And do you have faith in yourself?

## Being stuck doesn’t mean you need someone with all the answers

This is easy to forget. When we feel stuck, we often assume someone else must know the answer. Sometimes they do. But sometimes the answer isn’t something another person can simply give you. You may need to figure out what you want before you can figure out what to do.

A guide can help you work through that. A mentor can help you understand the road ahead. Both can be valuable, and they simply offer different kinds of support. Whoever you choose, look for someone who listens before trying to fix. Rushing to make you feel better isn’t the same as understanding you. Sometimes the most helpful response is simply: “I’m glad you told me.”

## What should good guidance leave behind?

This is where my own view comes in. Good guidance should leave you better able to guide yourself afterward. I don’t think the goal should be that you need someone every time you face an important decision.

A guide can help you develop better ways of thinking, recognize patterns, challenge assumptions, and turn an idea into a plan. But eventually you make decisions without them. That isn’t a failure. It is one of the signs that the guidance did something useful. It probably won’t be easy, and I believe growth rarely comes from the easy parts.

## So, which is better when you feel stuck?

There isn’t one answer. If you mainly want to learn from someone else’s experience, a mentor may be the better choice. If you need help understanding your own situation, making decisions, or finding direction, a spiritual guide may be a better fit. And if you want both advice and space to think, you may not need to choose a label at all.

The better question isn’t “Which one is better?”. It is “What kind of help do I actually need right now?”
