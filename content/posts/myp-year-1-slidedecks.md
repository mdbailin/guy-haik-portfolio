# MYP Year 1 Slide Decks Built for Thinking, Practice, and Reflection

Good teaching materials do more than place information on a screen. They create a rhythm for the lesson: orient students, give them something to think about, make space to try, and then pause to reflect on what they have learned.

The **MYP Year 1 mathematics slide decks** are designed around that rhythm. They provide a coherent sequence for the year while leaving room for students to explain, question, make connections, and solve problems in their own ways.

## Start with the curriculum map

The best place to understand the structure of the materials is the [IB MYP Mathematics Curriculum Map](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1QnWSTHUj95ZkpCW7dzpeFoO-WnHiJ9pF8EldCU9y61E/edit?usp=drivesdk). It shows how the units and topics fit together across the year, so individual decks can be used as part of a planned progression rather than as isolated lessons.

The [MYP Year 1 slide-deck repository](https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1kY1PdkwcjteoQrAX-sw6CcqWm9sAg0qF?usp=drive_link) contains the decks organized by unit. The sequence begins with number systems and operations, then develops through algebraic thinking, ratio and proportion, geometry and measurement, data and probability, and coordinates and transformations.

## A clear lesson arc

Each deck is organized to help a teacher move from intention to understanding. A typical sequence includes:

1. **Learning objectives** that make the destination visible to students.
2. **MYP framing** that connects the lesson to key and related concepts, as well as relevant assessment criteria.
3. **Explanations and worked examples** that model the mathematical idea without making the lesson purely lecture-based.
4. **Guided practice and problems** that let students apply the idea while the teacher can notice misconceptions.
5. **Inquiry prompts and challenges** that ask students to justify, compare, generalize, or find more than one way to represent an idea.
6. **An exit ticket** that provides a concise check of understanding and a moment of reflection before the lesson ends.

This structure makes the decks useful as both a teaching guide and a student-facing learning path. The slides are not just a sequence of answers; they are prompts for mathematical activity.

## Inquiry activities that ask students to make meaning

Inquiry appears in the decks through questions that go beyond “calculate.” Students may be asked to create different descriptions of a number, decide which representation is most useful in a context, explain why a method works, or investigate what changes and what stays constant.

These prompts support the MYP emphasis on communication and reasoning. They give students a reason to talk about mathematics, not only perform it, and they create natural openings for pair work, class discussion, and written justification.

## Problems with purpose

The problem sets are varied enough to support progression. Some questions build fluency with a new skill; others require students to apply it in an unfamiliar situation or explain their reasoning. Worked examples establish a model, while later tasks ask students to take more responsibility for choosing a method and communicating a conclusion.

Because the problems are embedded in the lesson flow, teachers can use them flexibly: as guided examples, individual practice, discussion starters, extension tasks, or quick formative checks.

## Real-world scenarios that give mathematics a reason

The decks also place mathematical ideas in recognizable settings. Examples include representing local or global statistics, comparing quantities, working with areas and measurements, and making decisions about which form of information is most useful.

These contexts are not decoration. They help students see why a representation, operation, or model might matter. A number can be more than an answer on a page: it can describe a population, a distance, a space, a change, or a decision.

## Exit tickets that close the loop

The exit tickets offer a short end-of-lesson checkpoint. They often combine a few focused questions with a reflection prompt, allowing students to show what they can do and articulate what they understand.

For teachers, this creates useful evidence for the next lesson. The responses can reveal whether students need another representation, more practice with a particular procedure, or a deeper challenge. For students, the final pause reinforces that reflection is part of learning—not an optional extra after the “real” mathematics is finished.

## A resource that supports—not replaces—teaching

The decks are designed to give lessons a dependable structure while leaving the teacher room to respond to the students in front of them. A teacher can slow down at a misconception, extend an inquiry, change the grouping, or use a problem as the starting point for discussion.

That balance is the value of a strong slide deck: it reduces preparation friction without turning teaching into pressing “next.” The curriculum map provides the year-long architecture; the individual decks provide the lesson-level pathway; and the inquiry, problems, real-world scenarios, and exit tickets keep students doing the thinking.
